Flood planning for homeowners

Is your home ready for the floods of 2055?

Rising seas and heavier rain are making floods more frequent every year. Plug in your home's flood risk and see what it would cost to elevate, insure, or relocate, and which choice holds up over the life of a 30-year mortgage.

Why flood risk is changing

1%

The "100-year flood" myth

A 100-year flood has a 1% chance of happening in any year, not once a century. Across a 30-year mortgage, that's roughly a 1 in 4 chance of at least one flood, before climate change is even counted.

↑

The water line keeps moving up

Each inch of sea-level rise lifts every future flood. Because big floods are only slightly taller than small ones, a modest rise can make flooding several times more common. A home with a 1% yearly risk today could face 3% by the time the loan is paid off.

×

Three ways to cut your risk

Flood risk is hazard × exposure × vulnerability. You can't stop the water, but elevating lowers your exposure, insurance limits your financial vulnerability, and relocating takes you out of harm's way.

Hazard today

FEMA's "Special Flood Hazard Area" (the 100-year floodplain) is the zone with at least a 1% yearly chance. 0.2% is the edge of the 500-year floodplain; 10% means a 10-year flood reaches your floor.

Climate change

NOAA projects about 1 ft of sea-level rise along U.S. coasts between 2020 and 2050 (more on the western Gulf Coast). 0 ft means no climate change. The rise is added gradually, year by year.

Your location

In flat coastal areas, a rare flood is only a little higher than a common one, so a small rise in sea level makes floods much more frequent. Where floods vary a lot in height (steep river valleys), the same rise matters less. Every foot of rise multiplies flood frequency by 101/this number.

Your plan

Lifting the home onto a taller foundation puts the living floor above more floods. It costs a lot up front, but it also lowers your insurance premium for as long as you own the home.

What drives the results

The later years matter most

With no water-level rise, elevating rarely pays for itself. At 2–3 ft of rise, the uninsured and insured cost lines curve sharply upward toward the end of the 30 years, while the elevated home's line stays nearly flat. A risk that's rare when you buy can become routine before you sell.

Location changes everything

One foot of rise is a much bigger deal where the 10-year and 100-year floods are close in height (try 0.5 ft) than where they're far apart (try 5 ft). The same climate change can mean very different outcomes for two homes a few miles apart.

Insurance is protection, not a bargain

The insured line usually runs slightly above the uninsured one, because premiums include a markup over expected claims. What you're buying is protection from a single $50,000–$100,000 repair bill (see the last column of the table). Most mortgage lenders also require flood insurance in the 100-year floodplain.

Elevation buys safety first, savings second

Each foot of elevation cuts flood odds by the same factor that a foot of water-level rise multiplies them. Elevating more than the expected rise leaves you safer than you are today. But if your starting risk is low, the up-front cost may never pay off. Try the 0.2% (500-year) setting.

Methodology

  1. Flood chance each year. Floods higher than a given level get rarer by a factor of 10 for every d feet of height (d = the location slider). That's the steep tail of a standard (Gumbel) flood-frequency curve. In year t the water has risen by R·t/T (R = rise slider, T = years you stay), so the yearly flood rate is
    rate(t) = rate₀ × 10^((R·t/T − raise) / d), with annual chance = 1 − e^(−rate).
  2. Chance of at least one flood over the whole stay = 1 − Π(1 − chance each year). With no climate change and a 1% yearly chance: 1 − 0.99³⁰ = 26%.
  3. Damage per flood. How deep the water gets above the floor follows the same curve (average depth = d / ln 10). Each depth is turned into a percent of the building's value using the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers depth–damage curve for a one-story house without a basement (e.g. 1 ft → 23%, 3 ft → 40%, 6 ft → 59%).
  4. Insurance. Covers damage up to the National Flood Insurance Program limit of $250,000 for the building, minus the deductible. The premium is the expected payout each year plus the markup, so it rises as the risk rises (FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 prices policies by actual risk).
  5. Elevating. One up-front cost (base + cost per foot), then lower flood chances and lower premiums from then on.
  6. Moving. Selling costs (agent fees, closing costs, moving) plus the price cut buyers demand for a flood-prone house, paid once. After that, no flood risk. You can't fully escape the risk by selling, because the next owner prices it in.

Simplifications: costs aren't discounted for inflation or interest; at most one damaging flood per year; contents (furniture, cars) aren't included; the price cut for flood risk is a fixed percentage and doesn't grow as the risk grows. Real decisions also involve safety, stress, and being displaced while repairs happen.
