Raise it, insure it, or move? A flood-risk explorer for homeowners

You own a house in a floodplain and plan to stay for the next 30 years. Sea levels and heavy rain are rising, so floods that used to be rare are becoming common. Should you spend money now to raise the house, pay every year for flood insurance, or sell and move? Move the sliders to see how the answer changes.

The problem in plain language

A "100-year flood" isn't once a century

It means a 1% chance every year. Over a 30-year mortgage, that adds up to about a 1 in 4 chance of being flooded at least once, even if the climate stayed the same.

The baseline is rising

Higher seas and heavier downpours lift every flood a little higher. A small rise in water level makes floods that reach your floor many times more frequent, so this year's 1% house could be a 3% house by the time the mortgage is paid off.

Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability

You can't stop the water (the hazard). But raising the house cuts your exposure, insurance cuts your financial vulnerability, and moving removes both.

Set up your situation

The first three sliders describe the flood threat. The last one is your decision.

Hazard today

FEMA's "Special Flood Hazard Area" (the 100-year floodplain) is the zone with at least a 1% yearly chance. 0.2% is the edge of the 500-year floodplain; 10% means a 10-year flood reaches your floor.

Climate change

NOAA projects about 1 ft of sea-level rise along U.S. coasts between 2020 and 2050 (more on the western Gulf Coast). 0 ft means no climate change. The rise is added gradually, year by year.

Your location

In flat coastal areas, a rare flood is only a little higher than a common one, so a small rise in sea level makes floods much more frequent. Where floods vary a lot in height (steep river valleys), the same rise matters less. Every foot of rise multiplies flood frequency by 101/this number.

Your decision

Lifting the house onto a taller foundation puts the floor above more floods. It's expensive up front, but it also lowers your insurance premium for the rest of the mortgage.

What to notice when you move the sliders

Climate change rewards acting early

Set the water-level rise to 0 ft and raising the house rarely pays off. Push it to 2–3 ft and the "do nothing" and "insurance only" lines bend sharply upward in the later years, while the raised house stays flat. The rising baseline turns a rare risk into a routine one within a single mortgage.

The sliders work together

The same 1 ft of rise does far more damage where the 10-year and 100-year floods are close in height (try 0.5 ft) than where they're far apart (try 5 ft). The hazard slider's effect depends on your location, and the raise slider works against both of them.

Insurance costs more than it pays out, on average

The insured line usually sits a little above the uninsured line, because insurers charge more than they expect to pay. Insurance isn't about saving money on average. It protects you from a single $50,000–$100,000 bill you might not survive financially (see the last column of the table). Most lenders require it in the 100-year floodplain anyway.

Raising the house beats the flood, not the bill

Each foot of height divides the flood chance by the same factor the water-level rise multiplies it by. Raising by more than the expected rise keeps you safer than you are today. But with a low starting risk, the up-front cost may never be earned back. Try the 0.2% (500-year) setting.

How the numbers are calculated

  1. Flood chance each year. Floods higher than a given level get rarer by a factor of 10 for every d feet of height (d = the location slider). That's the steep tail of a standard (Gumbel) flood-frequency curve. In year t the water has risen by R·t/T (R = rise slider, T = years you stay), so the yearly flood rate is
    rate(t) = rate₀ × 10^((R·t/T − raise) / d), with annual chance = 1 − e^(−rate).
  2. Chance of at least one flood over the whole stay = 1 − Π(1 − chance each year). With no climate change and a 1% yearly chance: 1 − 0.99³⁰ = 26%.
  3. Damage per flood. How deep the water gets above the floor follows the same curve (average depth = d / ln 10). Each depth is turned into a percent of the building's value using the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers depth–damage curve for a one-story house without a basement (e.g. 1 ft → 23%, 3 ft → 40%, 6 ft → 59%).
  4. Insurance. Covers damage up to the National Flood Insurance Program limit of $250,000 for the building, minus the deductible. The premium is the expected payout each year plus the markup, so it rises as the risk rises (FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 prices policies by actual risk).
  5. Raising. One up-front cost (base + cost per foot), then lower flood chances and lower premiums from then on.
  6. Moving. Selling costs (agent fees, closing costs, moving) plus the price cut buyers demand for a flood-prone house, paid once. After that, no flood risk. You can't fully escape the risk by selling, because the next owner prices it in.

Simplifications: costs aren't discounted for inflation or interest; at most one damaging flood per year; contents (furniture, cars) aren't included; the price cut for flood risk is a fixed percentage and doesn't grow as the risk grows. Real decisions also involve safety, stress, and being displaced while repairs happen.
